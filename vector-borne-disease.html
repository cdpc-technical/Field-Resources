Vector-Borne Disease Reference

Vector Control · Field Reference

This chart lists the diseases and conditions transmitted by mosquitoes, ticks, and fleas in the United States, along with the species that carry them. Use it to answer customer questions accurately, to justify service recommendations, and as a training reference for vector control programs.

Coverage is limited to pathogens and conditions with established or documented transmission in the United States and its territories. Travel-acquired illnesses without local vector transmission are not included.

Technicians Do Not Diagnose

Never tell a customer they have, or do not have, a disease. Never interpret symptoms. Refer all health questions to a physician and all pet health questions to a veterinarian. Speak in terms of risk and vector capability, not outcomes.

01

Mosquito-Borne

Roughly 200 mosquito species occur in the United States, but only a handful drive disease transmission. Container breeders such as Aedes and Culex are the species most often produced on a customer's own property, which is why source reduction carries more weight than adulticiding alone.

Disease Primary Vectors U.S. Distribution Signs and Symptoms Field Notes
West Nile Virus PriorityWest Nile virus (flavivirus) Culex pipiens, Cx. quinquefasciatus, Cx. tarsalis All 48 continental states. The most commonly reported mosquito-borne illness in the country. About 8 in 10 infections cause no symptoms. Fever, headache, body aches, and rash in most symptomatic cases. Less than 1 percent develop neuroinvasive disease. Culex breed in stagnant, organically rich water: catch basins, clogged gutters, neglected pools, tarps, livestock troughs. Peak risk August into September. Biting activity runs dusk to dawn.
La Crosse Encephalitis PriorityLa Crosse virus (orthobunyavirus) Aedes triseriatus (eastern treehole mosquito) Upper Midwest, Appalachian, and mid-Atlantic regions. Tennessee, West Virginia, North Carolina, and Ohio report the majority of cases. Most severe cases occur in children under 16. Fever, headache, nausea, lethargy, and in severe cases seizures. Breeds in tree holes and artificial containers: tires, buckets, birdbaths, tarps, corrugated downspout extensions. Container elimination is the highest-impact control step on a residential account.
Eastern Equine EncephalitisEEE virus (alphavirus) Culiseta melanura maintains the bird cycle. Bridge vectors to people include Coquillettidia perturbans, Aedes, and Culex species. Atlantic and Gulf coastal states, plus the Great Lakes region. Rare but among the most severe arboviral illnesses. Fever, headache, and encephalitis. Roughly 30 percent of cases are fatal and survivors often have lasting neurologic damage. Tied to hardwood swamps and freshwater cedar bogs, so risk is landscape driven rather than yard driven. Risk climbs late summer after amplification in bird populations.
St. Louis EncephalitisSLE virus (flavivirus) Culex species, including Cx. quinquefasciatus, Cx. nigripalpus, and Cx. tarsalis Scattered across the southern, central, and western states. Most infections cause no illness. Fever and headache when symptomatic. Adults over 60 face the highest risk of encephalitis. Same breeding sources and same control approach as West Nile virus. Both often circulate in the same areas at the same time.
Jamestown Canyon VirusJamestown Canyon virus (orthobunyavirus) Multiple Aedes and Culiseta species Reported most often in the upper Midwest and Northeast, but detected broadly. Fever, fatigue, headache. Occasionally progresses to meningitis or encephalitis. Deer serve as the amplifying host. Transmission window runs spring through fall, earlier in the season than West Nile virus.
Western Equine EncephalitisWEE virus (alphavirus) Culex tarsalis, Aedes melanimon Western and central states. No confirmed human cases in decades, but the virus persists in the bird cycle. Fever, headache, and in rare severe cases encephalitis. Infants face the highest risk. Associated with irrigated agriculture and flood-irrigated pasture. Horses are affected far more often than people.
DengueDengue virus, four serotypes Aedes aegypti, Aedes albopictus Endemic in Puerto Rico, the U.S. Virgin Islands, American Samoa, and Guam. Periodic local transmission in Florida, Texas, Hawaii, Arizona, and California. High fever, severe headache, pain behind the eyes, joint and muscle pain, rash. Severe dengue is a medical emergency. Both vectors are daytime biters that rest in shaded vegetation and under decks. They breed in small containers holding as little as a bottle cap of water.
ChikungunyaChikungunya virus (alphavirus) Aedes aegypti, Aedes albopictus Mostly travel-associated. Limited local transmission documented in Florida and Texas. Abrupt fever with severe joint pain that can persist for months. Same vectors and same source reduction plan as dengue and Zika. One container program addresses all three.
ZikaZika virus (flavivirus) Aedes aegypti, Aedes albopictus. Also transmitted person to person. Local transmission occurred in Florida and Texas during 2016 and 2017. Territories remain at risk. Often mild or absent. Rash, fever, joint pain, red eyes. Infection during pregnancy can cause severe birth defects. Customer concern spikes with any news coverage. Keep the response factual and focused on container elimination and harborage reduction.
MalariaPlasmodium species (protozoan parasite) Anopheles quadrimaculatus in the East, An. freeborni in the West Nearly all cases are travel-associated. Small clusters of locally acquired cases occurred in Florida, Texas, Maryland, and Arkansas in 2023. Cyclical fever, chills, sweats, headache, fatigue. Anopheles prefer clean, sunlit standing water such as pond margins and ditches rather than the foul water Culex use.
Dog Heartworm PriorityDirofilaria immitis (nematode) Over 20 species across Aedes, Culex, and Anopheles Reported in all 50 states. Heaviest along the Gulf Coast, the Southeast, and the Mississippi River Valley. Dogs and cats only. Cough, exercise intolerance, weight loss, and eventually heart failure. The most useful talking point with pet owners because the risk is immediate, local, and familiar. Recommend the customer keep their veterinary preventive current alongside mosquito service.

Mosquito Program Reminders

  • Source reduction outranks every other tactic. One overlooked container can out-produce an entire barrier treatment.
  • Inspect and document: gutters, corrugated downspout extensions, tires, tarps, toys, plant saucers, birdbaths, boat covers, wheelbarrows, unused pools, French drains, and low turf that holds water.
  • Treat resting harborage on the underside of foliage, dense shrub interiors, under decks, and shaded structural voids.
  • Log every breeding source and conducive condition in the FieldRoutes service note so the next tech sees the same picture.
02

Tick-Borne

Tick species determines disease risk more than any other factor, so correct identification matters. A single tick can also carry more than one pathogen. Most transmission comes from nymphs, which are about the size of a poppy seed and are routinely missed during self-checks.

Disease Primary Vectors U.S. Distribution Signs and Symptoms Field Notes
Rocky Mountain Spotted Fever PriorityRickettsia rickettsii Dermacentor variabilis (American dog tick), D. andersoni (Rocky Mountain wood tick), Rhipicephalus sanguineus (brown dog tick) in Arizona and along the southern border Despite the name, most reported cases come from North Carolina, Tennessee, Missouri, Arkansas, and Oklahoma. Sudden fever, severe headache, and a rash that often starts on the wrists and ankles. Can be fatal within days without prompt treatment. The highest-consequence tick illness in most of our footprint. Brown dog tick involvement means peridomestic infestations tied to dogs and kennels, not just wooded lots.
Alpha-gal Syndrome PriorityIgE allergy to galactose-alpha-1,3-galactose. Not an infection. Amblyomma americanum (lone star tick) Southeast, South Central, and mid-Atlantic, matching the lone star tick's expanding range. Delayed allergic reaction, typically 3 to 6 hours after eating mammalian meat. Ranges from hives and stomach upset to anaphylaxis. Increasingly common across our service area and often the reason a customer calls. Treat it as a real motivator, not a curiosity, and never speculate about whether a customer has it.
Ehrlichiosis PriorityEhrlichia chaffeensis, E. ewingii Amblyomma americanum (lone star tick) Southeast, South Central, and mid-Atlantic states. Fever, chills, severe headache, muscle aches, and sometimes a rash. More common in the Southeast than Lyme disease. Lone star ticks are aggressive questers that actively pursue a host, so customers notice them. All three life stages bite people.
Lyme DiseaseBorrelia burgdorferi, B. mayonii Ixodes scapularis (blacklegged or deer tick) in the East and upper Midwest, Ixodes pacificus on the West Coast Concentrated in the Northeast, mid-Atlantic, and upper Midwest, with pockets in northern California and Oregon. Confirmed cases in the Southeast are comparatively rare. An expanding rash in most cases, plus fever, fatigue, and joint pain. Untreated infection can cause arthritis and neurologic or cardiac complications. Transmission generally requires 36 to 48 hours of attachment, which makes prompt removal genuinely protective. Nymphal activity peaks May through July.
AnaplasmosisAnaplasma phagocytophilum Ixodes scapularis, Ixodes pacificus Northeast, upper Midwest, and parts of the West Coast. Fever, chills, severe headache, muscle aches. Older adults face higher risk of severe illness. Shares a vector with Lyme disease and babesiosis, so coinfection in a single bite is possible.
BabesiosisBabesia microti (protozoan) Ixodes scapularis Northeast and upper Midwest. Malaria-like illness with fever, chills, sweats, and fatigue. Can be severe in older adults, immunocompromised patients, and people without a spleen. White-footed mice are the reservoir, so rodent harborage reduction supports the tick program.
Powassan VirusPowassan virus (flavivirus) Ixodes scapularis, Ixodes cookei Northeast and Great Lakes region. Rare but frequently severe. Fever, headache, weakness, and encephalitis. Lasting neurologic effects are common in survivors. Can transmit in as little as 15 minutes of attachment, which is the strongest argument for immediate tick removal rather than waiting.
Heartland and Bourbon VirusesHeartland virus, Bourbon virus Amblyomma americanum (lone star tick) Midwest and Southeast, with cases reported in Missouri, Tennessee, Kansas, and Oklahoma. Fever, fatigue, headache, muscle aches, with low white blood cell and platelet counts. No specific treatment exists. Both are recently described and rare, but they occur in our region. Worth knowing by name if a customer raises them.
Southern Tick-Associated Rash IllnessCausative agent not confirmed Amblyomma americanum (lone star tick) Southeast and South Central states. A circular rash resembling Lyme disease, plus fatigue, headache, and muscle aches. Explains many Lyme-like rashes reported in the Southeast where blacklegged ticks are uncommon. Do not offer this as an explanation to a customer, refer them to a physician.
TularemiaFrancisella tularensis Dermacentor variabilis, D. andersoni, Amblyomma americanum. Also deer flies and direct contact with infected rabbits and rodents. Reported nationwide, concentrated in Arkansas, Missouri, Kansas, and Oklahoma. Ulcer at the bite site, swollen lymph nodes, fever. Other forms occur depending on route of exposure. Also a wildlife handling risk. Wear gloves and use a sealed bag for any dead rabbit or rodent removal, and never handle carcasses bare-handed.
Rickettsia parkeri RickettsiosisRickettsia parkeri Amblyomma maculatum (Gulf Coast tick) Gulf Coast and southern Atlantic coastal states. A dark scab at the bite site, fever, headache, and rash. Generally milder than Rocky Mountain spotted fever. Gulf Coast ticks favor tall grass and coastal prairie edges rather than deep woods, so mowing and edge management matter.
Colorado Tick FeverColorado tick fever virus Dermacentor andersoni (Rocky Mountain wood tick) Rocky Mountain states, typically above 4,000 feet elevation. Fever, chills, headache, and body aches, often returning in a second wave after a brief recovery. Activity peaks spring into early summer at elevation.
Tick-Borne Relapsing FeverBorrelia hermsii and related species Ornithodoros soft ticks Western mountain states, strongly associated with rodent-infested rustic cabins. Repeating cycles of high fever separated by symptom-free intervals. Soft ticks feed for minutes at night and drop off, so the customer usually never sees a tick. Control is rodent removal plus exclusion of the nesting voids.
Tick ParalysisNeurotoxin in tick saliva. Not an infection. Dermacentor species most often, plus several others Nationwide. Weakness that starts in the legs and moves upward. Most often affects children and pets. Symptoms resolve after the tick is removed. Reinforces full-body checks after outdoor exposure. Attached ticks are frequently found at the hairline and behind the ears.

Watch Item: Asian Longhorned Tick

Haemaphysalis longicornis is an invasive species now established across much of the eastern United States. No human pathogen transmission has been confirmed domestically to date, but it reproduces without mating and can build enormous populations on a single host. It is a serious livestock and equine concern. Report unusual heavy tick loads on animals to your branch manager.

Tick Program Reminders

  • Ticks concentrate at habitat edges. Focus on the transition between turf and woods, fence lines, leaf litter, ground cover, stone walls, and wood piles.
  • Rodent and deer activity drive tick populations. Note wildlife pressure and harborage during every inspection.
  • Customer guidance: tuck pants into socks, use repellent, shower within two hours of exposure, and perform a full-body check the same day.
  • Removal method: fine-tipped tweezers close to the skin, steady upward pull, no twisting, no heat, no petroleum jelly.
  • Advise customers to note the date and to contact a physician if fever or rash develops after a bite.

Flea-Borne

The cat flea, Ctenocephalides felis, is responsible for the overwhelming majority of flea infestations in the United States on both cats and dogs. Flea-borne disease is far less common than tick-borne disease here, but the wildlife and rodent connection makes it a real consideration on accounts with rodent pressure or feral animal activity.

Disease Primary Vectors U.S. Distribution Signs and Symptoms Field Notes
Flea-Borne Typhus PriorityRickettsia typhi. Also called murine typhus. Xenopsylla cheopis (oriental rat flea), Ctenocephalides felis (cat flea) Most cases reported from Texas, California, and Hawaii, with the range appearing to expand. Fever, headache, body aches, and rash. Rarely fatal but can require hospitalization. Transmitted through infected flea feces scratched into the bite, not the bite itself. Rats, opossums, and feral cats sustain the cycle, so rodent and wildlife exclusion is the real control.
Flea-Borne Spotted FeverRickettsia felis Ctenocephalides felis (cat flea) Scattered nationwide, likely underreported because it resembles flea-borne typhus. Fever, headache, rash, and sometimes a scab at the bite site. Carried by the same flea species found on the average household pet, which makes routine flea control the practical preventive.
PlagueYersinia pestis Oropsylla montana (ground squirrel flea) and other wild rodent fleas Rural western states: New Mexico, Arizona, Colorado, California, Nevada, Oregon, and Utah. A handful of cases occur each year. Sudden fever, chills, weakness, and a swollen painful lymph node. Septicemic and pneumonic forms are more severe. Treatable with prompt antibiotics. In endemic areas, treat for fleas before removing rodents. Killing the host first drives infected fleas to look for a new one. Never handle dead rodents without gloves.
Cat Scratch DiseaseBartonella henselae Cat fleas spread the bacterium between cats. People are infected through scratches or bites from a carrier cat, or by flea dirt entering a wound. Nationwide, most common in the Southeast and in warm humid regions where fleas thrive year round. A bump at the scratch site, swollen tender lymph nodes, fever, and fatigue. Kittens are the most common source. Flea control on the animal is the prevention step. A strong reason to insist on veterinary flea treatment alongside any structural flea service.
Flea TapewormDipylidium caninum (cestode) Cat and dog fleas, which must be swallowed to transmit the parasite Nationwide wherever fleas occur. Primarily pets. Rice-like segments near the tail or in bedding. Rare in children who swallow an infected flea. The most familiar flea-related problem to pet owners. Useful for explaining why a partial flea job is not a finished flea job.
Flea Allergy DermatitisAllergic reaction to proteins in flea saliva. Not an infection. Ctenocephalides felis (cat flea) Nationwide, and the leading cause of skin disease in dogs and cats. Intense itching, hair loss along the lower back and tail base, scabbing, and secondary skin infection. A few bites can trigger a reaction. Explains why a sensitized pet still shows symptoms at very low flea counts. Set that expectation up front so the customer does not read it as service failure.

Flea Program Reminders

  • Structural treatment without veterinary treatment on the animal will not resolve an infestation. Both are required and the customer needs to hear that at the first visit.
  • Target where the pet rests, not just open floor: bedding, furniture bases, carpet edges, along baseboards, and under low furniture.
  • Pupae are protected inside the cocoon and emerge over the following weeks. Continued adult activity after service is expected, not a failure.
  • Customer prep drives the outcome: thorough vacuuming, laundering pet bedding on hot, and vacuuming again daily for two weeks to trigger emergence.
  • Check for wildlife under crawl spaces, decks, and sheds. Untreated rodents, opossums, or feral cats will reseed the account.
