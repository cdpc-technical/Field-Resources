General Pest Protocol
Yellow Jackets
Locate and eliminate yellow jacket colonies through direct dust treatment of the nest entrance, supported by residual perimeter application and food source reduction. Yellow jackets sting repeatedly and are the most aggressive stinging pest we treat - technician safety and correct timing drive this service.
PEAK JUL - OCT

1
Arrival and Intro

Park in the road if possible, or on the side of the house. Clock in to app in FieldRoutes. Knock and ring the doorbell (Mandatory).

Ask where they are seeing activity and whether they have located an entry point in the ground, a wall, or a void. Ask whether anyone has been stung, whether anyone in the household has a known sting allergy, and where children and pets spend time in the yard. Ask them to keep everyone away from the area until you have finished.

If anyone reports a sting reaction, direct them to seek medical attention. Yellow jacket stings cause anaphylaxis in sensitized individuals and a single yellow jacket can sting repeatedly. Do not assess a sting or offer medical guidance - refer them to a physician.
2
Identification and Biology

Yellow jackets are the most aggressive stinging pest we treat and account for the majority of serious sting incidents. Understanding the nest type tells you how to approach the treatment.

Appearance

Roughly 1/2 inch, stout bodied, with sharply defined bright yellow and black banding. Smooth and largely hairless, unlike honey bees. Wings fold lengthwise at rest. Fast, erratic fliers that hover and dart.

Nesting

Most commonly nests underground in abandoned rodent burrows, but also in wall voids, soffits, attics, crawlspaces, under decks, and inside meter boxes and equipment. A single small entry hole with steady traffic is the giveaway.

Colony Cycle and Aggression

Colonies are annual. A single overwintered queen starts the nest in spring and the colony builds through the season, peaking in late summer and early fall when it may hold thousands of workers.

Aggression peaks in late season as natural food sources decline. This is when yellow jackets become scavengers at trash cans, picnics, and outdoor dining, and when sting incidents spike.

They do not lose their stinger and can sting repeatedly. Disturbing a ground nest can trigger a mass defensive response within seconds.

Distinguish From Honey Bees Before Treating. Honey bees are hairy, amber and brown rather than bright yellow, and are far less aggressive. If there is any chance the insect is a honey bee, stop and refer to the Honey Bee Protocol before applying anything.
3
Inspection and Nest Location

You must locate the nest entrance before treating. Treating foragers accomplishes nothing - the colony continues producing workers.

Locating the Nest

Stand back at a safe distance and watch flight patterns. Workers will move in a consistent line to and from a single entry point. Follow that line back to its origin.

Ground nests: Look for a small hole in the ground, often in bare or sparse turf, along bed edges, at the base of trees, in slopes and embankments, or under landscape timbers and stone. Traffic in and out is steady and obvious once you spot it.

Structural nests: Check soffit and fascia gaps, siding seams, weep holes, utility and plumbing penetrations, attic and gable vents, under decks and steps, and inside meter boxes, grills, and unused equipment.

Note whether the nest sits in a high-traffic area - near a door, walkway, play area, or where the customer mows.

Do not probe, poke, or disturb the entrance during inspection. Identify it visually from a distance. Never place a hand, tool, or face near a suspected entry point before treatment.
4
Treatment

Treat the nest directly. Dust is the preferred material for both ground and void nests because workers track it deep into the nest structure on their bodies.

🕑 Timing

Treat at dusk, after dark, or in early morning whenever practical. The full colony is present and inactive, and workers are far less responsive.

Midday treatment leaves foragers in the field who will return to an untreated entrance, and provokes a much stronger defensive response.

Where daytime treatment is unavoidable, plan a clear exit path before you approach and do not treat in a confined space with no retreat.

🌪 Ground Nest Treatment

Apply D-Fense Dust directly into the entrance hole using a bulb duster with an extension tip. Deliver a generous application into the opening.

Do not plug or cover the hole immediately. Workers must move through the treated entrance to carry dust into the nest.

Step back promptly and observe from a distance. Return to confirm activity has ceased before considering the nest resolved.

D-Fense Dust (Deltamethrin) - CSI
🌪 Void and Structural Nest Treatment

Apply D-Fense Dust into the void through the entry point using a duster with an extension tip. Treat soffit voids, wall voids, and attic voids at the point of entry.

Do not seal the entry point at time of treatment. Sealing traps live workers who will chew or push through into interior living space. Advise the customer to wait until activity has fully ceased, then seal.

D-Fense Dust (Deltamethrin) - CSI
💧 Aerosol Knockdown and Perimeter

Where immediate knockdown of exposed workers is needed for safe access, use a contact aerosol before dusting.

Following nest treatment, apply a liquid residual around the nest entry area, along the foundation, and around doors, windows, eaves, and soffits to reduce foraging and discourage new nesting.

Temprid FX (Beta-Cyfluthrin, Imidacloprid) - Envuor Suspend PolyZone (Deltamethrin) - Envu
PPE is not optional on a yellow jacket nest. Wear a bee suit or full protective gear, veil, and gloves for any direct nest treatment. Plan your retreat before you approach. If the nest is in a location that cannot be treated safely - high on a structure, inside a confined attic space, or in an area you cannot exit quickly - stop and escalate to your Service Manager.
5
Customer Recommendations

Prevention centers on removing food attraction and closing off nesting opportunities before spring.

RecommendationWhy It Works
Keep trash cans sealedLate season yellow jackets are aggressive scavengers. Tight fitting lids and clean bins remove the primary attraction.
Clean up food and drinks outdoorsSugary drinks, open cans, and food left out draw foragers directly to where people are sitting.
Pick up fallen fruitRotting fruit under trees is a major late season food source.
Rinse recycling containersResidual sugar in cans and bottles attracts foragers to the bin area.
Seal structural gaps in early springCaulk siding seams, screen attic and gable vents, and seal utility penetrations before queens begin nesting.
Fill abandoned rodent burrowsGround nests almost always use an existing burrow. Filling them removes the nesting site.
Do not swat or crush themA crushed yellow jacket releases an alarm pheromone that recruits nestmates to the area.
Suggested Customer Talk Track

"These are yellow jackets, and unlike a honey bee they can sting you over and over, so I want everybody staying clear of that area tonight. I found the nest entrance and treated it directly - the workers carry that material back down inside, which is what takes out the colony. Give it a few days and watch for traffic to stop. Don't plug that hole yet, because they need to keep moving through it. And this time of year they're scavenging hard, so keeping lids tight on the trash makes a real difference."

6
Post Service

Complete a detailed service report in FieldRoutes documenting the nest location and type, treatment time of day, product applied, and whether activity was still present at departure.

Report to the customer, emphasizing that the area should be avoided until activity ceases and that the entry point must not be sealed yet. Get a signature on the iPad and leave a review card.

Ensure all back yard gates are secure and all equipment is loaded in your vehicle.

Setting Expectations and Follow-Up

Advise the customer that activity typically declines over 24 to 72 hours as workers contact the treated entrance. Returning foragers will continue arriving for a day or two.

Advise them to stay clear of the nest area until traffic has fully stopped, and to seal the entry point only after that.

If activity continues beyond a week, schedule a follow-up. Large late season colonies occasionally require a second treatment.

Colonies die out naturally over winter and nests are not reused, but the same nesting locations attract new queens each spring.
