General Pest Protocol
Millipedes
Reduce millipede intrusion through granular and liquid treatment of exterior harborage, targeted entry point dusting, and correction of the moisture and organic debris conditions that sustain them. Millipedes cannot survive indoors and do not damage the structure.
YEAR ROUND

1
Arrival and Intro

Park in the road if possible, or on the side of the house. Clock in to app in FieldRoutes. Knock and ring the doorbell (Mandatory).

Ask where they are seeing millipedes and roughly how many. Ask whether the activity started after a heavy rain or a change in weather. Ask about irrigation schedules, recent mulch installation, and any drainage problems. Ask which side of the home is worst - this is usually the shaded or low-lying side.

2
Identification and Biology

Millipedes are a classic occasional invader. They cannot survive indoors and do not feed on the structure, its contents, or people. Setting that expectation early defuses most of the customer's concern.

Appearance

Elongated, cylindrical, hard-bodied, brown to blackish. Typically 1 to 1.5 inches. Two pairs of short legs per body segment - this distinguishes them from centipedes, which have one pair per segment and much longer legs. Coils into a tight spiral when disturbed or dead.

Key Behavior

Feeds on decaying organic matter - leaf litter, mulch, thatch, and rotting wood. Slow moving and nocturnal. Requires constant moisture and dies within days indoors. Migrates in large numbers after heavy rain, during prolonged wet periods, or when their habitat dries out suddenly.

Why They Come Inside: Millipedes do not want to be indoors. They migrate when their habitat becomes saturated after heavy rain or when it dries out abruptly. Mass invasions are weather driven and often occur in a short burst over a few days.
Customer Note: Millipedes do not bite or sting. Some species secrete a defensive fluid that can stain surfaces and irritate skin, so advise the customer to sweep or vacuum them rather than handle them barehanded.
3
Inspection

Millipede control is an exterior job. Focus your inspection on the moisture and organic debris ringing the structure.

Exterior Priority Areas

Mulch and landscape beds: Pull mulch back from the foundation and check underneath. Deep mulch against the wall is the single most common source. Note the depth.

Leaf litter and debris: Accumulations in beds, along fence lines, in window wells, and in corners where wind collects material.

Turf and thatch: Heavy thatch layers hold moisture and organic matter. Check where turf meets the foundation.

Wood and stone: Firewood piles, landscape timbers, stepping stones, decorative rock, and any wood-to-soil contact.

Drainage: Downspout discharge points, low spots that hold water, poor grading toward the structure, and over-irrigated zones.

Entry points: Foundation cracks, weep holes, siding starter strip, door thresholds, garage door seals, basement windows and window wells, and crawlspace vents.

Interior

Interior activity concentrates near the entry point - along baseboards on the affected side, at door thresholds, in garages, basements, and crawlspace access areas.

Most millipedes found indoors will already be dead or dying. Large numbers of dead millipedes inside confirms a heavy exterior population and an open entry route.

4
Exterior Treatment

The exterior barrier and granular application are the core of this service. Treat the harborage, not just the wall.

🌾 Granular Application

Apply granule around the structure, walking 7 to 9 ft from the foundation, rotating the torso as you turn the handle. Avoid cement walkways, driveways, patios, and decks.

Focus heavily on landscape beds, mulch, groundcover, and any area holding leaf litter or organic debris. Granule is critical here because it carries down into the mulch and thatch layer where millipedes actually live.

Wisdom EZ or Wisdom G L/N (Bifenthrin) - per seasonal rotation
💧 Power-Spray Barrier

Treat the foundation 3 ft up the exterior wall and 4 ft out on the ground. Spend adequate time - a light pass will not hold against a mass migration.

Fan spray landscape beds heavily so product reaches 1 to 2 inches below the surface. Treat a minimum of 4 ft out and up to 10 ft out where beds are deep.

Pin stream along the foundation and treat around all doors, thresholds, garage door seals, weep holes, basement windows, window wells, utility penetrations, and crawlspace vents.

Suspend PolyZone (Deltamethrin) - Envuor Bifen IT (Bifenthrin) - CSI
🌪 Crack and Crevice Dusting

Dust foundation cracks, weep holes, behind the siding starter strip, crawlspace vents, and gaps below siding or stucco.

D-Fense Dust (Deltamethrin) - CSI
5
Interior Treatment

Interior treatment is light and targeted. Millipedes indoors are dying already, so the goal is to handle the ones actively entering rather than broadcast the interior.

💧 Targeted Application

With the B&G on a fine fan spray, treat along baseboards on the affected side, at door thresholds, around garage perimeter and door seals, and along basement sill plates and window wells.

Advise the customer to sweep or vacuum up accumulated dead millipedes. Removing them prevents secondary pest issues and gives an accurate read on whether new activity is occurring.

Onslaught FastCap (Multi Active) - MGK
6
Customer Recommendations - Moisture and Habitat

Millipede pressure is entirely driven by moisture and organic debris around the foundation. These changes are what prevent the next migration event.

RecommendationWhy It Works
Thin mulch to 2 inches or lessDeep mulch is the primary millipede habitat and moisture reservoir. Thinning it removes both food and shelter directly at the entry point.
Pull mulch back from the wallA 6 to 12 inch bare gap between mulch and the foundation breaks the bridge into the structure.
Remove leaf litter and debrisLeaf accumulation in beds, window wells, and along fences is a direct food source and breeding habitat.
Correct drainageExtend downspouts, clear gutters, and regrade low spots so water moves away from the foundation rather than pooling against it.
Adjust irrigationOver-watering beds adjacent to the home sustains millipede populations year round. Water less frequently and earlier in the day.
Dethatch heavy turfA thick thatch layer holds moisture and decaying organic matter directly against the structure.
Move firewood and timbersStore firewood off the ground and well away from the home. Remove or replace rotting landscape timbers.
Seal entry pointsInstall or replace door sweeps and garage door seals, caulk foundation cracks, and repair window well and vent screens.
Suggested Customer Talk Track

"Millipedes live in the mulch and leaf litter around the house, and after all that rain they got pushed out and headed for the first dry thing they found, which is your foundation. They can't survive inside - the ones you're finding are already dying. I treated the beds and the foundation today. The real fix is getting that mulch thinned out and pulled back off the wall, and making sure the downspouts are carrying water away from the house."

7
Post Service

Complete a detailed service report in FieldRoutes documenting the sides of the structure affected, mulch depth and drainage conditions observed, entry points found, and all treatment locations.

Knock on the door and report to the customer, emphasizing that millipedes cannot survive indoors and that habitat changes at the foundation are what prevent recurrence. Get a signature on the iPad and leave a review card.

Ensure all back yard gates are secure and all equipment is loaded in your vehicle.

Setting Expectations

Advise the customer they will continue to find millipedes for several days after treatment, particularly if a migration event is underway. Dead millipedes indoors are the treatment working.

Mass migrations are weather driven. Heavy rain, prolonged wet periods, or sudden drying can trigger a new push even after a good treatment. Homes with heavy mulch or drainage problems should expect repeat pressure until conditions are corrected.

Schedule a follow-up if live activity continues beyond two weeks or if a new weather event drives another migration.
