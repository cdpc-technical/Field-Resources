General Pest Protocol
Migrating Honey Bees
Correctly identify honey bees, distinguish a temporary migrating swarm from an established colony, and route each to the proper response. Honey bees are a protected beneficial pollinator - swarms are referred to beekeepers and are never treated, and colony treatment requires management authorization.
PEAK APR - JUN

Stop and Identify Before Anything Else

Honey bees are a protected, beneficial pollinator and are treated differently from every other stinging insect we service. Before any product comes off the truck, confirm what you are actually looking at.

Misidentifying a honey bee swarm as yellow jackets and treating it is a serious error with reputational, regulatory, and potentially legal consequences. When in doubt, stop and call your Service Manager.

BodyFuzzy and hairy, amber to golden brown with darker bandsSmooth and shiny, bright yellow and black or black and white

If it is a honey bee, do not treat. Proceed through this protocol to determine the correct disposition. Applying insecticide to a honey bee colony or swarm is prohibited under ClearDefense policy except in the narrow circumstances described in Step 4, and only with management authorization.
2
Swarm or Established Colony

The single most important determination on a honey bee call is whether you are looking at a transient swarm or an established colony. The response is completely different.

Migrating Swarm - Temporary

A dense cluster of bees hanging on a branch, fence, mailbox, vehicle, or structure. No comb, no wax, no honey. The cluster is resting while scout bees look for a permanent home.

Swarming bees are at their most docile - they have no nest or brood to defend and are engorged with honey. Most swarms move on within a few hours to a couple of days on their own.

Established Colony - Permanent

Bees entering and exiting a fixed point in a wall void, soffit, chimney, tree cavity, or outbuilding, with wax comb present. Steady directional traffic at a single opening.

May involve substantial comb and stored honey inside a structural void. Requires physical removal, not treatment - see Step 5.

Swarm Season: Swarming peaks in spring and early summer, typically April through June, as colonies reproduce and split. A swarm call during this window is expected and usually resolves itself. This is the most common honey bee call we receive.
3
Correct Response to a Migrating Swarm

Do not treat a swarm. Do not spray it, dust it, knock it down, or hose it off. A resting swarm is the easiest and most valuable situation for a beekeeper to collect, and it is very often gone on its own before any action is needed.

What To Do

Leave it alone. Advise the customer that the swarm is temporary and typically departs within a few hours to two days once scouts select a permanent site.

Advise the customer to keep people and pets clear of the immediate area and to avoid disturbing, spraying, or throwing anything at the cluster. A disturbed swarm is far more likely to sting.

Refer to a local beekeeper. Most state beekeeping associations and county extension offices maintain a free swarm removal list, and beekeepers will usually collect a swarm at no charge. Provide the customer with that referral path.

Notify your Service Manager or GM so the branch is aware of the call and the referral, and document it in FieldRoutes.

Where the swarm sits in a genuinely high-risk spot - a doorway, a school, a play area, or where someone with a known sting allergy is present - escalate to your GM immediately rather than acting on your own.

Set the Customer at Ease. Customers are often alarmed by the size of a swarm cluster. Explaining that swarming bees are docile, that they have nothing to defend, and that they will move on shortly resolves most of these calls without any treatment at all.
Suggested Customer Talk Track

"What you've got here is a honey bee swarm - they're in the middle of moving house. There's no nest and no honey there, they're just resting while their scouts find a permanent spot. Honestly this is the calmest bees ever get, because they've got nothing to protect. Nine times out of ten they'll be gone on their own within a day or two. I'd just keep everybody clear and don't spray or disturb them. If you'd like it gone sooner, I can point you to a local beekeeper - they'll usually come collect a swarm for free because it's a healthy colony they can use."

4
When Treatment May Be Considered

Treatment of honey bees is a last resort and requires management authorization. It is never the response to a migrating swarm.

Required Conditions - All Must Be Met

1. It is an established colony, not a swarm. Comb is present and the colony is fixed in place.

2. Live removal is not feasible. A beekeeper or bee removal specialist has been contacted and declined, or the colony is inaccessible for live removal.

3. There is a documented health or safety threat. Colony is in an occupied structure with active interior intrusion, or a person on site has a documented sting allergy, or the location presents an unavoidable public exposure risk.

4. Management has authorized it. Your GM or Service Manager has approved the treatment in writing. Do not make this call in the field on your own.

5. State requirements are satisfied. Some states impose notification or restrictions on honey bee colony treatment. Confirm current requirements for your state with your GM before proceeding.

Never treat a honey bee colony without GM authorization. If a customer pressures you to spray bees on the spot, explain that honey bee treatment requires management approval and that you will get an answer for them. Do not be talked into it at the door.
Always Offer the Referral First. Live removal by a beekeeper or bee removal specialist is the preferred outcome in every case. It is better for the customer, better for the bees, and better for our reputation. Exhaust that option before treatment is even discussed.
5
Established Colony in a Structure - Removal Requirements

An established colony inside a wall void, soffit, or chimney is a construction problem as much as a pest problem. Explain this clearly to the customer.

Why Treatment Alone Fails

A mature colony can hold substantial comb and many pounds of stored honey. Killing the colony without removing the comb leaves that material in the void.

Unattended honey ferments, melts, and leaks through drywall and ceilings, causing staining and structural damage.

The abandoned comb and dead brood then attract secondary infestations - wax moths, carpet beetles, ants, roaches, and rodents.

The residual pheromone scent in the void attracts new swarms to the same location in following seasons unless the cavity is cleaned and sealed.

Correct Disposition

Refer the customer to a bee removal specialist or a beekeeper who performs cut-outs. This involves opening the void, removing comb and bees, cleaning the cavity, and sealing it.

Advise that a contractor may be needed for the repair, and that this work falls outside ClearDefense scope.

ClearDefense does not perform structural bee removal, cut-outs, or comb extraction. Do not commit to this work.

Where the customer proceeds with removal, we can treat the cavity for secondary pests and seal small entry points after the colony and comb are fully removed. Coordinate that timing with your GM.

Document Everything. Record the colony location, whether comb was observed, what referral was provided, and what the customer elected to do. Honey bee calls carry more liability than routine services and clear documentation protects both the technician and the branch.
6
Customer Recommendations - Prevention

Prevention centers on denying access to structural cavities that swarms find attractive.

RecommendationWhy It Works
Seal gaps before swarm seasonCaulk siding seams, trim gaps, and fascia joints ahead of spring so scouts cannot access wall voids.
Screen attic and gable ventsVents are a common entry to the large protected cavities swarms prefer.
Cap chimneysAn uncapped or unscreened chimney flue is a classic colony site.
Seal utility penetrationsGaps around electrical, plumbing, and HVAC entries provide direct void access.
Address previous colony sitesAny cavity that has held a colony must be cleaned and sealed, or residual scent will draw new swarms year after year.
Do not spray beesReinforce that homeowners should never spray a swarm or colony themselves. It provokes defensive behavior and kills a protected pollinator.
7
Post Service

Complete a detailed service report in FieldRoutes documenting the identification made, whether it was a swarm or established colony, comb presence, the referral provided, and any management authorization obtained.

Report to the customer, explaining the identification, why treatment is not the response, and the referral path. Get a signature on the iPad and leave a review card.

Ensure all back yard gates are secure and all equipment is loaded in your vehicle.

Key Reminders

A swarm is temporary and docile. Leave it alone and refer to a beekeeper.

An established colony requires physical removal, not treatment, and falls outside our scope.

Treatment requires all five conditions in Step 4 and written GM authorization. Never make that call alone in the field.

When you cannot confidently identify what you are looking at, photograph it, do not treat, and escalate to your Service Manager or Technical Manager.
