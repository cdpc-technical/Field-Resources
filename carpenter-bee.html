General Pest Protocol
Carpenter Bees
Eliminate active carpenter bee galleries through direct dust application and reduce future boring activity with broadcast liquid protection on vulnerable wood surfaces. Effective control depends on treating the galleries directly, not just killing the bees you can see.
APR - JUN PEAK
Field Identification
Carpenter Bee

1
Biology and Identification

Understanding carpenter bee biology is what allows you to explain the service confidently and set correct expectations with the customer. Know these facts cold.

Male Carpenter Bee

Has a distinctive white or yellow patch on the face. Males have no stinger and cannot sting. They are the aggressive-looking ones that hover and dive-bomb at people near the nest - this is purely territorial bluffing behavior and is completely harmless.

Female Carpenter Bee

Solid black face, no patch. The female is the one that bores the galleries. She can sting but is extremely docile and will generally only sting if physically handled or trapped against skin. She is focused on excavating and provisioning, not defending.

Carpenter Bee vs. Bumble Bee: Carpenter bees have a shiny, hairless black abdomen. Bumble bees have a fuzzy, hair-covered abdomen with yellow markings. Carpenter bees nest in wood; bumble bees nest in the ground or in cavities. This distinction matters - bumble bee colonies are treated differently and are important pollinators.
2
Arrival and Intro

Park in the road, if possible, or on the side of the house. Clock in to app in FieldRoutes. Knock and ring the doorbell (Mandatory).

Ask the customer where they have seen bee activity and where they have noticed holes or sawdust. Ask how long they have been seeing activity and whether they have noticed any woodpecker damage. Ask whether any areas of the home have been repainted or restained recently, and whether any wood surfaces have been left bare.

3
Inspection

Inspect the full exterior of the structure with a flashlight, working top to bottom. Carpenter bees favor bare, weathered, and unpainted softwood on the underside of horizontal surfaces where they are protected from rain.

Priority Inspection Areas

Underside surfaces: Eaves, soffits, fascia boards, and the underside of overhangs. These are the most common attack points because the bee is sheltered from weather while boring.

Decks and railings: The underside of deck railings, handrails, joists, stair stringers, and the underside of deck boards. Pressure-treated wood is still vulnerable once it has weathered.

Structural trim: Window and door trim, corner boards, wooden shutters, and any exposed dimensional lumber.

Outbuildings: Sheds, barns, pergolas, playsets, fencing, and any unpainted wood structure on the property. These are frequently the actual source population feeding activity on the main structure.

Roofline: Rake boards, gable ends, and any wooden vent framing.

Signs of Activity

Round entry holes approximately 1/2 inch in diameter. Coarse sawdust on surfaces or ground directly below holes. Yellow or brown staining streaking down from the hole - this is fecal staining and indicates an active, provisioned gallery. Male bees hovering in a defended territory near the gallery. Woodpecker damage around existing holes, indicating larvae are present inside.

Count and Document: Record the number of active holes and their locations in FieldRoutes. This gives you a baseline to measure against on follow-up visits and supports the conversation with the customer about progress.
4
Gallery Dust Treatment

Direct dust application into the gallery is the primary control method. This is what kills the adult female, the developing larvae, and any bee that returns to or emerges from the gallery. Liquid spray alone will not resolve an active infestation.

🌫️ D-Fense Dust - Application

Using a hand duster with an extension tip, inject dust directly into each active entry hole. Insert the tip into the opening and apply enough dust to coat the interior gallery walls - typically two to three short puffs per hole. The goal is a light residual coating throughout the tunnel, not a packed plug of dust.

Treat every hole found, including holes that appear old or inactive. Galleries are reused across seasons and an apparently abandoned hole frequently contains overwintering adults or developing larvae.

Work methodically across the structure so no holes are missed. Use a ladder to safely reach eave and soffit galleries - do not skip elevated holes because they are inconvenient to access.

D-Fense Dust (Deltamethrin) - CSI
🔧 WD-40 - Breaking Down Wax Seals

Female carpenter bees seal off provisioned brood cells within the gallery using a wax and chewed wood partition. These seals can block dust from penetrating deeper into the tunnel and reaching developing larvae.

Where a sealed or plugged gallery is encountered, WD-40 may be applied into the hole first to break down the wax partition, allowing dust to travel further into the gallery on application. Apply the WD-40, allow it a moment to work, then follow with the D-Fense Dust application.

WD-40 (Wax Seal Breakdown - Optional)
Do not plug or seal the holes. Leaving galleries open allows returning and emerging bees to continue contacting the dust residual, which is what carries control through the rest of the season. Sealing a hole traps the residual away from the target and can drive bees to bore a new exit through adjacent wood.
5
Broadcast Liquid Application

Following the dust treatment, apply a broadcast liquid application with the power sprayer to vulnerable wood surfaces. This provides a residual barrier that deters new boring activity and treats bees landing on the surface to begin excavating.

💧 Power Sprayer - Coverage Areas

Treat all bare and weathered wood surfaces where boring activity is likely: eaves, soffits, fascia, rake boards, gable ends, deck railings and undersides, exposed joists, window and door trim, corner boards, and any wooden outbuilding surfaces on the property.

Apply thorough coverage to the underside of horizontal surfaces - this is where the majority of boring occurs and where a spray application is most often applied too lightly. Angle the application upward to properly wet these surfaces.

Do not neglect outbuildings, fencing, and playsets. These frequently hold the source population that is pressuring the main structure.

Pollinator Protection: Avoid drift onto flowering plants and blooming vegetation. Carpenter bees are legitimate pollinators, and non-target bee species are protected. Treat wood surfaces only - never apply to blooms or foliage in flower. Be mindful of wind conditions.
Timing Matters: The most effective time for broadcast application is early spring as adults emerge and begin seeking nesting sites - typically April through June in our markets. Treatment applied before boring begins prevents the damage rather than reacting to it.
6
Service Scope - What ClearDefense Does Not Provide

Be clear and confident with the customer about the boundaries of our service. Setting these expectations up front prevents dissatisfaction later.

We Do Not Seal Holes

Sealing galleries is not part of our service and is not recommended immediately after treatment. Open holes keep the dust residual accessible to returning and emerging bees. Sealing also traps live bees which will bore a new exit through adjacent wood. Filling holes is a customer repair task to be done later in the season, after activity has ceased.

We Do Not Offer Carpenter Bee Traps

ClearDefense does not sell, install, or service carpenter bee traps. If a customer asks about them, explain that our treatment program targets the galleries and the wood surfaces directly, which addresses the source rather than intercepting a portion of the adult population. Traps are available to customers at retail if they choose to use them on their own.

7
Home Improvement Recommendations for the Customer

Chemical treatment manages the current population. Long-term prevention comes from making the wood unattractive to bore into. These recommendations give the customer real, actionable ownership of the outcome and are worth communicating on every carpenter bee service.

🎨 Paint and Finish - The Single Best Prevention

Carpenter bees strongly prefer bare, weathered, unfinished wood. A quality oil-based paint is the most effective long-term deterrent. Stain provides some reduction but is significantly less effective than paint. Clear sealers and polyurethane provide minimal protection.

Advise the customer to prioritize painting the surfaces where activity was found - especially eaves, soffits, fascia, and the underside of railings. Maintaining that finish over time is what keeps those surfaces protected.

🪵 Material Selection for Repairs and Replacement

When wood needs replacement, recommend hardwoods, composite or PVC trim, fiber cement, or vinyl over bare softwood. Carpenter bees rarely attack these materials. If softwood must be used, it should be painted before installation - including cut ends and the back face.

🔨 Filling Holes - Timing and Method

Advise the customer to wait until activity has ceased for the season - typically late fall - before filling holes. Filling too early traps live bees inside, which will bore out through adjacent wood and create new damage.

When filling, recommend wood putty, caulk, or wooden dowels driven into the hole and sanded flush, then painted over. Filling and painting prevents that gallery from being reused the following spring and blocks water intrusion into the wood.

🏠 General Property Maintenance

Replace any wood showing rot or significant weathering, as deteriorated wood is easier to bore and more attractive. Ensure gutters and drainage direct water away from wooden trim to slow weathering. Address any woodpecker damage promptly, as opened galleries accelerate moisture intrusion and decay.

Suggested Customer Talk Track

"The bees you're seeing hovering around are the males, and they can't sting - they're just being territorial. What we've done today is treat inside every hole we found, which handles the female and the larvae developing in there, plus we've treated all your bare wood surfaces to keep new bees from starting. The biggest thing you can do on your end is paint those bare wood areas - an oil-based paint makes a huge difference because they really only want unfinished wood. And hold off on filling those holes until the fall, once activity stops, so we don't trap anything inside."

8
Customer Education - Biology Talking Points

Customers are often alarmed by carpenter bee behavior and confused about the damage. These are the facts most worth sharing on site. Being able to explain the biology builds confidence and reduces callbacks driven by normal seasonal behavior.

Fact What to Tell the Customer
They do not eat wood Carpenter bees bore into wood to create nesting galleries, not to feed. They feed on nectar and pollen and are actually valuable pollinators. The sawdust below a hole is excavated material, not consumed wood.
Perfectly round 1/2 inch hole The entry hole is almost perfectly round and about the diameter of a finger. This is a signature sign - no other wood-destroying insect leaves a hole this clean and uniform.
Galleries run with the grain The bee bores straight in about an inch, then makes a sharp turn and tunnels with the wood grain. Galleries can extend 6 to 10 inches and much further in reused nests.
Galleries are reused and extended Females frequently return to existing galleries year after year and extend them. A gallery reused over many seasons can run several feet through a structural member. This is why annual treatment matters.
Males cannot sting The aggressive hovering bee that buzzes people is the male, and he has no stinger. This reassurance alone resolves most customer anxiety about the pest.
They prefer untreated softwood Bare, weathered, unpainted softwood is the primary target - cedar, redwood, cypress, pine, and fir. Painted or hardwood surfaces are far less attractive to them.
Secondary damage from woodpeckers Woodpeckers hear and target the larvae inside galleries. The resulting damage from a woodpecker excavating a gallery is frequently far more extensive and costly than the original bee damage.
Moisture and decay follow Open galleries allow water intrusion into structural wood, which leads to rot and creates conditions attractive to other wood-destroying organisms. The damage compounds over time if left untreated.
One generation per year Adults emerge in spring, mate, and the females bore and provision galleries. New adults emerge in late summer, feed, and overwinter inside galleries to start the cycle again the following spring.
9
Post Service

After the service is complete, fill out a detailed service report in FieldRoutes documenting the number and location of holes treated, surfaces broadcast treated, and all recommendations made to the customer.

Knock on the door and report to the customer, emphasizing what you did and what you found. Walk them through the biology talking points and home improvement recommendations. Get a signature on the iPad and leave a review card.

Ensure all back yard gates are secure and all equipment is loaded in your vehicle.

Setting Follow-Up Expectations

Advise the customer they may continue to see some bee activity for a period after treatment as bees return to treated galleries and contact the residual. This is expected and is the treatment working. Activity should decline noticeably over the following weeks.

If new holes appear or activity remains heavy, a follow-up visit should be scheduled to treat newly bored galleries. Carpenter bee pressure is heaviest in spring, and accounts with significant bare wood may require more than one visit during the peak season.
